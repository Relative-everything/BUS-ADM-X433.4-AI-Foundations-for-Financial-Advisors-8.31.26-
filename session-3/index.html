01 and 02 · Mechanism7 min

Meaning Space, and the Failure It Creates

Wolfram's definition: an embedding turns a word into an array of numbers, and words with similar meanings get nearby numbers.

The numbers come from company, not meaning. Wolfram measures how similar the surroundings are in which words appear, across five billion words of web text, and each word becomes a long list of numbers: 768 for GPT-2, 12,288 for GPT-3. A retrieval tool runs the same nearest-neighbour test on your documents.

Wolfram's own example holds the failure. "Alligator" and "crocodile" appear in almost interchangeable sentences, so they sit close together; "turnip" and "eagle" do not, so they sit far apart. Alligators and crocodiles are different animals. What was measured is whether two words keep the same company, not whether they refer to the same thing, and the Cole file has exactly that hazard: its documents use near-identical language and differ in one condition each.

Sounding alike is the signal. Being the same thing is what you needed.

03 · RAG, mechanically6 min

Retrieval Mechanics: Chunking, Indexing, and Ranking

Retrieval-augmented generation, RAG for short, makes the model answer from your documents instead of from memory. Four steps, none of them mysterious.

  • Split. The documents are cut into short passages.
  • Index. Each passage gets a numeric fingerprint, the kind §01 showed.
  • Rank. Your question gets the same fingerprint, and the passages closest to it rank highest.
  • Answer. The top few passages are pasted into the prompt and the model is told to answer only from them.

For the Coles the documents are the 2011 will, trust, power of attorney and healthcare directive, the 2014 buy-sell, the 2023 appraisal, an unsigned planning memo and two unsigned drafts, cut into ten passages. The tool is not asked to know the family. It is asked to read the passages the ranker hands it, so everything that goes wrong goes wrong in the ranking or in the reading. Two of the four presets below are built to fail, and nothing on screen says so.

Do You Need Retrieval at All?

Most planning files fit in one prompt. Anthropic's own guidance: under roughly 200,000 tokens, about 500 pages, put the whole set of documents in and skip retrieval.

  • Paste or attach the documents when the whole set fits. Everything the model needs is in front of it, and nothing has to be found first.
  • Use a retrieval tool when the set is far bigger than one prompt: a firm's whole archive, years of email. Then the finding step exists, and so do its failures (§04).
  • Either way, tell it to answer only from the documents and to say so when the answer is not in them.

The context window is the model's working space for one exchange: everything you attach plus the whole conversation so far, and all of it counts H.

05 · Two ways to teach it your file5 min

Grounding Compared With Fine-Tuning

Grounding changes what the model reads. Fine-tuning changes what the model is. For a planning practice, grounding wins, because your facts change and need citing.

  • Grounding. You hand the model the documents each time and tell it to answer from them. Change the file and the next answer changes. Every answer can point at a passage.
  • Fine-tuning. The model is retrained on your documents so they live inside it. Change the file and nothing changes until you retrain. Nothing can be cited.

Wolfram makes the point without either word: tell the model something once, as part of the prompt, and it will use it. The limit he names is that it can only take in what sits simply on top of what it already knows. Your house style might be worth tuning. Your facts are not.

07 · The note-taker7 min

How an AI Note-Taker Works, and How to Prompt It

A note-taker does four things in order: it transcribes, it extracts, it categorises, and it drafts the follow-up. The first is nearly solved. The second is where a model decides which sentences from ninety minutes become the record.

  • Before the meeting. Get consent to record. Add the tool to the call, or plan to upload the recording afterwards. Tell it what the summary must contain.
  • During. Talk. The tool listens and does not need managing.
  • After. Read the summary against the transcript before it reaches the client or the file. Then send the recap and log the note.

Kitces measures why the category exists: the average advisor spends more than an hour on prep and follow-up for every hour in client meetings H. The tool is sold against that hour.

Recording Consent and the Confidentiality Obligation

Requires instructor verification before it is taught as settled This section characterises recording law and a professional duty. Verify before teaching either as settled:
  • 18 U.S.C. § 2511 as a federal one-party-consent floor, with states free to require every party's consent.
  • The duty of confidentiality as it applies to handing client content to a documentation vendor, under your firm's policy and your credentialing body's code.

A note-taker is a recording device with a vendor attached. Two obligations follow: may the recording be made at all, and what may the vendor see once it is.

  • Consent. Federal law lets one party to a conversation record it H. Some states require every party's consent, and the published counts of those states disagree. Ask everyone on the call, every time, and note that you did.
  • Confidentiality, and what the vendor holds. The Cole file holds things one person must not learn: a competitor's approach that Nathan has not been told about. A vendor's system is a disclosure to everyone with access to it, so where the file lives, who can open it, how long it is kept and whether it trains a model are contract questions rather than preferences.

10 · Documentation6 min

Documenting Circumstances and the Basis for a Recommendation

A record of what was said is not a record of why you recommended what you recommended. The note-taker produces the first. The second you write yourself, because the alternatives you rejected were never spoken aloud.

  • Circumstances. The client's facts, in their words, dated: what changed, what they asked, what they want.
  • Basis. Which options you considered, why you chose this one over each of the others, and what the client said when you presented it.
  • Neither. Your reading of what the client feels or wants. An inference is not a record; label it or leave it out.

Written on the day, the basis is worth keeping. Rebuilt after a question arrives, it is worth almost nothing.

A prompt that builds the basis by asking you: the interview rewrite

Session 2 called this reverse prompting: the model interviews you before it drafts. It refuses to draft until it has the alternatives. Copy it into your own tool after a meeting.

ROLE
You are a compliance reviewer at an SEC-registered investment adviser.
You do not draft the record. You interrogate me until the record is complete.

TASK
Build the basis-for-recommendation entry for one recommendation I made.

PROCEDURE
Ask me one question at a time. Do not draft anything until you have all four
elements below. If I answer vaguely, ask again with a narrower question.

1. CIRCUMSTANCE - the specific client fact that made this recommendation
   apply to this household and not to a generic household.
2. ALTERNATIVES - the other courses of action I actually considered.
   If I name fewer than two, ask what I ruled out and why it was ruled out.
3. SELECTION REASON - what made the recommendation preferable to each
   alternative named, stated as a comparison and not as a virtue.
4. CLIENT RESPONSE - what the client said when it was presented,
   including any disagreement or deferral.

CONSTRAINTS
- Do not invent any fact. If I have not told you something, ask for it.
- Do not add a rationale I did not give you.
- Do not state or imply any tax or legal conclusion. If a question turns on
  one, say so and stop.
- Flag any element I supplied that is an assertion rather than an observation.

OUTPUT
When all four are complete: one paragraph per element, under 90 words each,
then a list of every element where my answer was thin.
