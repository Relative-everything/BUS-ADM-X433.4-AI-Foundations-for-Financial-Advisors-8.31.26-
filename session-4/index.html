Deepfakes: one hard number and a pile of soft ones

In January 2024 the engineering firm Arup lost approximately $25 million across 15 transfers after an employee joined a video conference populated by synthetic versions of the CFO and colleagues, reported by the Financial Times in May 2024.H That is the only deepfake number here I would put in front of a client.

The rest is softer, and the softness is the lesson. Gartner, surveying 302 security leaders: 62% of organisations experienced at least one deepfake attack in twelve months, 37% on a live video call.M Deloitte projects US generative-AI-enabled fraud losses rising from $12.3 billion in 2023 to $40 billion by 2027 — a projection, not a measurement.M And two outlets citing the same Surfshark 2026 analysis report $2.19 billion and $3.7 billion.L

Two numbers, one underlying study, a 69% discrepancy. Neither outlet is lying. They are counting different things and neither says which.

Use Arup. If you need a market figure, say published totals range from roughly $2 billion to $4 billion depending on inclusion criteria, that all undercount, and that you do not know which is right. That sentence is more defensible than any number in it.

The control is not detection. Humans spot high-quality synthetic media at close to chance, and detectors lose much of their lab accuracy in the field.M What works is procedural: out-of-band verification on a channel the requester did not choose, applied to every disbursement instruction regardless of how convincing it was. Content Provenance and the SynthID Watermark

The previous section left you with a problem: you cannot detect a deepfake by looking at it. Provenance marking is the industry's answer. It works, it is deployed at scale, and it answers a narrower question than most people think it answers.

There are two ways to attach origin information to a file. Metadata bindings attach a cryptographically signed manifest alongside the content — this is the C2PA Content Credentials approach. In-content watermarks alter the content itself so the signal travels with the pixels, samples or tokens. SynthID is the second kind.

The distinction matters because metadata is separable from content. Standard C2PA manifests are lost when a non-C2PA-aware tool resaves a file, and platforms that re-encode on upload strip them silently, leaving no trace that a credential ever existed. A screenshot defeats them completely.H

A correction to the common framing

SynthID and C2PA are routinely compared as rivals, with watermarking scored as the winner. That framing is wrong and the specification says so. The C2PA specification defines a Durable Content Credential as a Content Credential for which one or more soft bindings exist — invisible watermarks and perceptual fingerprints that let a stripped manifest be recovered from a repository. Watermarking is a component of the C2PA architecture, not its competitor.H If you are asked to choose between them, the question is malformed.

The asymmetry that governs everything else

Presence is evidence. Absence is not.

A valid watermark or credential tells you something. Its absence tells you almost nothing, because there are many benign reasons for it: the content predates adoption, the generating model does not participate, the capture device does not sign, the file passed through a stripping pipeline, or a credential was simply never added. Most authentic content in circulation carries no provenance marking at all, so treating absence as suspicion produces an unusable false-positive rate.H

Note what this means for the tool this course requires you to use. Anthropic has not announced SynthID adoption for Claude, so nothing you generate in this course carries a SynthID watermark.M A detector returning "no watermark" on your own work is the expected result, not a finding.

Text: tournament sampling

The mechanism is published in Nature. A language model produces a probability score for each possible next token. Google's own example: for "My favourite tropical fruits are mango and…", the token bananas scores higher than airplanes. SynthID does not add anything to the text. It changes which token gets selected, by running a tournament among candidates in which each token also carries a pseudo-random value from a watermarking function seeded by the recent context plus a secret key.H

Detection re-runs the same tournaments with the same key. If the observed tokens win their brackets far more often than chance allows, the text is watermarked. No access to the original model is needed.H

The claim students find hardest to believe is that a pixel change can be both readable by a machine and invisible to a person. Amplify it and see.

Audio: hiding in the frequencies you cannot hear

SynthID embeds a watermark in audio generated through Lyria and the NotebookLM podcast feature. Google states it is inaudible and is not removed by adding noise, MP3 compression, or changing the speed of the track.H The technique exploits the same principle as image watermarking, applied to a different perceptual system: human hearing has frequency ranges of low sensitivity, and a loud sound masks a quieter simultaneous one. The signature goes there.

The practically important property is survival through the air. A signal encoded in the waveform rather than the file can survive being played on a speaker and re-recorded on a phone — the path a deepfaked voice instruction actually travels.

Scale: a figure worth not repeating

You will see a number attached to SynthID. Be careful which one. Google's announcement of the SynthID Detector portal cites over 10 billion pieces of content watermarked. Coverage of Google I/O in May 2026 reports more than 100 billion images and videos. Secondary sources published in the same month carry both figures.L Google's own current SynthID product page states no figure at all.H

Ten billion or one hundred billion, reported the same month, from the same company. A tenfold spread.

This is the deepfake-loss problem from the previous section repeating itself: a number gets a life of its own, propagates through secondary coverage, and arrives on your slide without a date or a definition attached. If you need to convey scale, say deployment is in the billions of items and cite the primary announcement you actually read.

Entropy, Regeneration and the Limits of Watermarking

Watermark capacity is bounded by how many acceptable outputs exist. Where only one output is correct, there is nowhere to hide a signal. This single constraint predicts every limitation that follows.

Google states the limitation directly: watermark application is less effective on factual responses, because there is less opportunity to augment generation without decreasing accuracy.H A model asked for the capital of France cannot select a lower-probability token to satisfy a watermark key without being wrong. The watermark and the correct answer compete for the same degree of freedom.

The advisor's inversion

Now map that onto what you actually produce. The outputs where a fabrication does the most damage — a citation, a tax figure, a formula, an allocation percentage — are precisely the lowest-entropy outputs, and therefore the least watermarkable. The outputs that watermark well are the ones where being wrong matters least.

Code and structured data are the extreme case. Executable code and spreadsheet formulas have almost no semantic slack: change a token and it either compiles differently or fails. Whatever slack remains sits in naming, quoting and whitespace — and the software ecosystem already destroys exactly that, automatically, every time a formatter or minifier runs. A code formatter parses to a syntax tree and reprints to a fixed standard, erasing lexical choices as a side effect of its normal job. Nobody has to attack the watermark; the build pipeline removes it.M

Regeneration

The other limit is adversarial. Passing a watermarked image through a second generative model re-synthesises the content, and the new pixels are drawn from a model that has no knowledge of the original key. Zhao et al. (2024) formalised this as a regeneration attack and showed invisible pixel-level watermarks are removable by generative AI; a 2026 analysis proves that as an image undergoes sufficient diffusion transformation, the mutual information between the image and the hidden payload approaches zero, and reports near-zero recovery against several deep watermarking schemes while preserving visual fidelity.H

But not uniformly, and the nuance is the useful part. Schemes that bind the signal to semantic or latent representations rather than superimposing it on pixels degrade far less: one 2026 scheme reports retaining 78.8% bit accuracy and 75% true-positive rate after diffusion purification.M Blanket claims that regeneration removes watermarks are wrong; it depends on where the signal is bound. Treat any single removal percentage you are quoted as scheme-specific until proven otherwise.

This session does not teach removal technique and none is given here. The finding matters defensively, and only defensively: it is the reason a compliance procedure must never treat a missing watermark as evidence of authenticity. Sources: Zhao et al. (2024) on regeneration attacks; arXiv 2602.20680 (Feb 2026) for the information-theoretic result; arXiv 2605.01479 for the semantic-binding counterexample. Christ et al. further prove that watermark undetectability requires cryptographic secrecy, so no scheme is secure against an adversary who holds the key.H

Hallucination Rates and the Verification Burden

Retrieval grounding reduces fabrication. It does not remove it. The best-measured professional research tools in an adjacent profession still returned incorrect information on roughly one query in six, and the worst on one in three, while marketing the opposite.

Magesh, Surani, Dahl, Suzgun, Manning and Ho, Hallucination-Free?, Stanford RegLab and HAI; preprint May 2024, published in the Journal of Empirical Legal Studies 2025. The first preregistered evaluation of proprietary retrieval-grounded legal AI, over 200 queries, expert hand-scored. LexisNexis had marketed "100% hallucination-free linked legal citations."H

Read the reduction honestly. Ungrounded to best-performing grounded: 43% to 17%, a 60% relative cut. To the worse-performing grounded tool: 43% to 33%, a 23% cut. Grounding is worth doing and is not a solution. The study also found the higher rate correlated with longer responses — roughly 350 words against 219 — so the more thorough-looking answer was the likelier one to contain something false.M

A measurement you cannot put on the same axis

Artificial Analysis currently measures Claude Opus 5 at a 50% hallucination rate, and places Claude Fable 5 top of the boards tracking how often a model is simply wrong, at 40 on AA-Omniscience.H Do not put that 50% next to the Stanford 43%. Different instruments, query sets and scoring rules, two years apart. A student who builds a slide comparing them has produced exactly the artefact this course exists to prevent. Both find double-digit error rates on their own terms; neither licenses a claim about the other.

Where the rate comes from, in one mechanism

Trained on balanced-parenthesis sequences, a small transformer assigns roughly 15% probability to a closing parenthesis at a position where one would necessarily leave the sequence unbalanced. Wolfram: this "isn't right, because that would necessarily lead to an unbalanced parenthesis." The model is not uncertain about a hard question. It is confidently assigning probability to a provably impossible continuation, because it estimates a distribution over next tokens rather than checking a constraint.H A fabricated citation is that same event with a legal authority in place of a bracket. Nothing in the architecture distinguishes them.

Audit Trails: Prompt, Output, Decision

FINRA asks for prompt and output logging, version tracking and access controls. The SEC asks whether your compliance team can explain how a tool reached a decision. Both reduce to one test: can another person re-run what you did and see why you concluded what you concluded.

Reproducibility is the hard part. Wolfram: "if we use the same prompt multiple times, we're likely to get different essays each time."H A record capturing only the prompt does not let anyone reproduce the output; one capturing only the output does not let anyone interrogate the reasoning. Four fields are the minimum: the exact prompt; the model identifier and its effort setting; the retrieved source text rather than a paraphrase; and the human decision with date and author.

Classification exercise

Six records, three categories. Two items are specific in form and defective in substance.

0 of 6 answered

A prompt block that produces the record

Paste at the end of any research prompt. It does not improve the answer. It changes what the answer contains, so the file you keep is a record rather than a conclusion.

Before your answer, output a header block containing exactly these fields: MODEL: the model identifier and effort or reasoning setting in use DATE: today's date PROMPT_HASH: the first eight words of my request, verbatim Then answer. After your answer, output a verification block containing: SOURCES: for each authority you relied on, quote the operative sentence verbatim and give its locator. Do not paraphrase. NOT_VERIFIED: every claim you made that is not supported by a quoted source above. List it even if you believe it is true. WOULD_CHANGE: what fact, if different, would change your answer. If you cannot quote a source verbatim, write NO SOURCE RETRIEVED for that item. Do not reconstruct a citation from memory.

Why the last instruction carries the weight. The failure mode is not a model that refuses to answer. It is one that produces a plausible citation having retrieved nothing, because producing plausible text is what it does. NO SOURCE RETRIEVED gives it a licensed output for that state. Whether a model honours it is empirical — which is why the block ends with something you can test rather than a promise you must trust.
