What the file records and no session resolves.

  • The stale designation. David's $210,000 rollover IRA still names his mother, Dorothy Cole, who died in 2021, with no contingent beneficiary (form dated 11 May 2009). By the custodian's default it would pay to his estate. It also blocks a clean backdoor Roth contribution for David (the aggregation rule); Meg, who holds no pre-tax IRA, is not blocked.
  • The 2011 documents. Two wills with no trust, a guardian clause for children of whom one is now an adult, and a successor agent who has died. The residence passes by joint tenancy; Illinois offers a transfer-on-death instrument the Coles have not used.
  • The 2024 memo and the 2026 plan. The prior adviser's Roth conversion memo (3 October 2024) says the 401(k) does not permit in-plan Roth conversion; the plan description amended 1 January 2026 says it does. Session 3's retrieval exercise grounds on both.
  • No long-term care coverage for either spouse, and Ruth's situation is the live example of the cost. David's group life ($370,000) ends at separation; Meg's $500,000 term policy ends in 2030.
  • The question that runs through every session. How much of the $1,720,000 of pre-tax money to convert to Roth, and in which years of the window, with the federal bracket in the year of conversion and the Medicare premium two years later as the two prices, and Illinois charging nothing.
