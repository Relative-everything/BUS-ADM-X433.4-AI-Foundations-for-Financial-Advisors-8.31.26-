The Coles and David’s Plan Change

Meg and David Cole, 58 and 59, are both W-2 earners in Naperville, Illinois: Meg is a nurse manager and David a senior mechanical engineer at Dunmore Industrial Equipment. They hold $2,446,000 of investable assets, 70.3% of it in pre-tax accounts. Meg plans to stop work in mid-2028 and David in late 2029. David’s 401(k) holds $1,150,000, of which $1,090,000 is pre-tax, and since 1 January 2026 his plan’s summary description lets him convert pre-tax money to Roth inside the plan.

How much to convert, and in which years, is the question that runs through the whole course, and this email does not answer it. It tells Meg and David what the rules are, so the conversation that follows starts from facts: what the plan now allows, how a conversion is taxed federally and in Illinois, and when required minimum distributions begin. Each statement carries the authority it rests on.

Both are drafted with an AI model and checked by the advisor before anyone else sees them. This file shows the draft with one citation planted wrong, the finished email, the log, how they were built, and the one slip the check caught.

The checks that were run

  1. Statements and authorities. A script compared statements A, B and C and each of their authority lines with the draft, character for character, and D’s three authority lines with the advisor’s check. Everything matched.
  2. New authorities. A script listed every section, statute, regulation and publication the email names and looked each one up in the pack. Everything traced; nothing was added.
  3. Figures, dates and years. A script pulled every number and year from the email and looked each one up in the pack. Everything traced.
  4. Derived numbers. Recomputed by hand: 1967 + 75 is 2042 and 1968 + 75 is 2043, and the two April dates follow from them.
  5. The added sentences. Each of the four plain sentences the model added was read against the pack and the case file. This is the step that caught the slip below, and a check that stops at the citations passes it.
  6. Confidences, amounts and length. Searched the email for the items the clients told the firm in confidence, for any dollar figure and for any word of advice about what to convert: none present. The email runs 278 words against about 250 asked.

The one failure, and how it was caught

From the email · Document 3A, under statement BFor both of you case file: David’s plan only, any amount converted would count as taxable income for that year, and the conversion could not be reversed.

Statement B is about the in-plan conversion, and the case file records that feature in David’s plan only (Parts B.2 and C.3.3). The pack never said Meg’s plan offers it. The model’s plain sentence widened the rule to both clients and told Meg that an irreversible conversion is one she can make. The authority line was right and the sentence under it was not, which is why a check of the citations passes it. Asking the model whether it was sure would not have caught it either: nothing in its own text contradicts the sentence.

How it was caught: check 5, by reading each added sentence against the pack. What changed: item 3 of the task had asked for “one plain sentence on what it means for the two of you”. It now asks the sentence to say whose plan or whose birth year it rests on, and says that the plan change is David’s plan only. Re-run with that change (capture e3, claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-12, prompt sha256 2fe0f5cc1694), the sentence read “If David converts, the amount counts as taxable income for that year, and he cannot reverse it afterward.”

  • Asked in one plain message to check the four citations in the draft and correct any that were wrong (capture e1, claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-12, prompt sha256 c43badc37d05), the model corrected D’s age, and correctly. Where the human decides

  • Which authority backs each statement. The advisor chose the four from the source list, and the standing instruction forbids a fifth. The model is told to write “authority not in pack” rather than supply one.
