  1. Figures. A script pulled every dollar amount, percentage and year from both drafts and looked each one up in the pack. Everything traced except the summary’s 13, and reading the sentence found the 15 beside it.
  2. Derived numbers. Recomputed by hand: 10% of $2,446,000 is $244,600 and 25% is $611,500. Both letter figures match.
  3. Year ranges. Counted each range with both ends included. This is the step that caught the failure below.
  4. Sources of claims. Every sentence about an event, a market or a document traced to a line of the pack. Nothing in either draft referred to anything the pack does not hold.
  5. Quotations. Both are word for word. Both are fragments, and Meg’s (“I never want to feel that.”) loses the sentence it points back to, so the advisor restores the full sentence before sending.
  6. Length. The summary ran about 480 words plus its table against 350 asked: over, and it prints on one page only at 9-point type. The letter ran 339 against about 350: within.
  7. Confidences. Searched both drafts for the three confidential items and for the balances of Meg’s mother: none present. The one hit for “debt” is the clients’ own stated goal, launching both children without debt.
  8. Both chairs. Read the letter as Meg and as David. Each gets the same four things in the same order, and the letter says plainly that neither view is wrong.

The one failure, and how it was caught

From the summary · Document 3A, lead fact 2… That is 2043 − 2028 = 15 yearscase file: 14 years for Meg and 2042 − 2029 = 13 yearscase file: 12 years for David, which sets the length of the conversion window (file: Meg 2029 to 2042, David 2030 to 2041, joint 2030 to 2041).

The model subtracted two years. The case file counts the window as the years with no wages and no required distribution, both ends included: 2029 to 2042 is 14 years and 2030 to 2041 is 12 (Part F.1). The arithmetic was right and the quantity was wrong, and the range quoted at the end of the sentence was correct, which is why the sentence reads as sound. Asking the model whether it was sure would not have caught this, because its own parenthesis already holds the right years.

How it was caught: check 3, by counting. What changed: the prompt had asked for “the years between each last paycheck and the first required distribution”, which invites a subtraction. Item 2 of Task A now asks for “the Roth conversion window for each spouse exactly as the file states it”. Re-run with that change (capture a3, claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-12, prompt sha256 19cd9b7a3cb6), the lead fact read “The Roth conversion window is Meg 2029 to 2042 and David 2030 to 2041”. The readme below carries the changed line.

Also hit, on the first plain requests

  • The letter (capture b1, claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-12, prompt sha256 186ebc475037) wrote a stress test nobody supplied, “Suppose stocks fall 40%, roughly what happened in 2008”, with a loss of “roughly $645,000”. It also turned the policy statement’s “has not been re-signed” into “neither of you has signed it”.
  • The summary (capture a1, claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-12, prompt sha256 488d1ae8f4ab) opened with a note that the file’s as-of date was later than “today’s date”, because it used the machine’s clock. It also restated the Dunmore holding as “about 2.5%” of investable assets where the file says 2%.
  • The standing instruction answers both: items 1 to 4 make the file the only source and forbid scenarios, and item 5 sets the date.

Where the human decides

  • What leads. The advisor chose the three lead facts and their order, and the prompt names them. Left to choose, the first plain draft (capture a1) put beneficiary and estate gaps first. Both orders are defensible, and choosing between them is the advisor’s job.
  • What stays out of the tool. An employer matter told in confidence, a relative’s loan and an adult child’s debt are in the client file and nowhere in the prompt. Whether and how to record them is the advisor’s decision.
  • A third party’s balances. Meg’s mother’s assets are left out for the same reason, so the summary says “Ruth’s savings: not in file” and lists the question for the next meeting.
  • The method in the letter. The five reconciliation steps are the model’s draft. The advisor owns the method and every promise in it, including the written policy both clients will be asked to sign.
  • The cuts. The summary is over length. Deciding what leaves the page is a judgment about the client, not a formatting task.
  • Sending. The advisor signs, after whatever review the firm requires.
