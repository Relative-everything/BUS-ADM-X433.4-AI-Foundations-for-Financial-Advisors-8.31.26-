The Coles, and the Workflow Behind Their One-Pager

Meg and David Cole, 58 and 59, plan to stop work in mid-2028 and late 2029. They hold $2,446,000 of investable assets, 70.3% of it in pre-tax accounts, and a mortgage of $165,200 at 3.10% that runs to November 2037. Their paychecks have paid for everything so far. When the paychecks stop, the firm gives them one page that says what comes in, when, and what stands behind it, and it redrafts the page whenever their numbers move.

The drafting follows a five-step workflow the firm wrote down, with an AI model at step 3. The workflow was written and tested on the model claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929, which Anthropic deprecated on 30 September 2026 and lists to retire on 30 November 2026 H. 1. Figures: no number outside the household file, and every number the task names is present.

4. Limits: nothing recommended, projected or derived.
5. Dates and windows: stop dates and conversion windows exactly as the file states them; count each window's years, first and last year both included.

The model, and why

The replacement is Sonnet 5.5 (claude-sonnet-5-5), the id Anthropic’s page names for claude-sonnet-4-5-20250929 H. The choice rests on changing one thing at a time: the same tier, the next generation, so that anything the log finds comes from the model and not from a tier change as well. Price did not decide it, since a draft costs a cent or two on any tier. Only Sonnet 5.5 was run on the replacement side, so this page makes no claim about what Haiku 5.5 or Opus 5.5 would have drafted.

The checks that were run

  1. Figures. A script listed every number in a draft and looked each up in the household file. None may sit outside the file, and each figure the task names must be there.
  2. Layout. A script counted the headings, looked for a title above the first, looked for bullets under each heading, and flagged any table or any line indented under a bullet.
  3. Length. A script counted the words against 300.
  4. Limits. A script flagged words such as “should” and “recommend”, and a person read the rest for a recommendation, a projection or a derived figure.
  5. Dates and windows. A script looked for the stop dates and the two windows exactly as the file writes them, and a person counted each window, first and last year both included: 2029 to 2042 is 14 years, and 2030 to 2041 is 12.

A draft passes at 5 of 5. Each row of the log is eight drafts, so the result is a count, and a single clean draft proves little about a model that can write eight different layouts.

The one failure, and how it was caught

From log row 2 · Document 5BSame prompt, eight drafts. Bullets nested a second level in 7 of 8 drafts, and in none of 8 on the old id. Figures, windows, length and limits clean in 8 of 8.

The loud break did not happen, and the date is why. On 2026-10-12 the old id still answered. Anthropic’s page says “requests to retired models will fail” H, so the baseline in row 1 could only be taken while the old id could be asked. After 30 November 2026 its answers cannot be reproduced, and the log is all that is left of them.

The quiet break. The swap changed one line of the workflow and nothing else. The new model got every figure right in all eight drafts, as the old one did, and it nested bullets in 7 of 8. The workflow never said “one level of bullets”, because the old model never needed telling; the rule existed only in the old model’s habits. Step 4 as it was written asks whether a draft looks right, and these drafts read well, so a reader had nothing to fail them on until someone wrote the rule down.

The first fix did not hold. Row 3 added one sentence, and 4 of 8 drafts still broke the layout: three nested again and one indented a continuation line. Reading one new draft and seeing it flat would have passed row 3. Running all eight through all five checks did not. Row 4 replaced the sentence with two that say where the bullets start and where a list of figures goes, and 8 of 8 passed on the replacement and 8 of 8 on the old id.

Also hit, on the looser wording

  • On the old id, 4 of 8 drafts added a title line or extra bold sub-headings, so v1 says “No title and no sub-headings”.
  • In row 1, 2 of 8 drafts on the old id wrote paragraphs where bullets were asked. The baseline is a measurement, not a pass, which is why it is logged before the swap.

Where the human decides

  • When to swap. The retirement date is public, so the advisor sets the swap and the re-test before 30 November 2026, while the old id can still give a baseline.
  • Which model. Same tier, next generation, one change at a time. Moving to another tier is a second change, with its own row.
  • What counts as a pass. The advisor set 5 of 5 as the line and the layout rule as the firm’s house style, one level of bullets for a one-page template. A different firm could choose differently; what the log owes is the sentence that enforces the choice.
  • What stays out of the tool. The case file records a second possible date for David’s last paycheck. It rests on something the clients told the firm in confidence, so the household file carries the planned date only and the page says “planned”. Whether and how the clients are told the date could move is the advisor’s decision.
  • What the page leaves out. The case file also holds the amounts at age 70 and the Medicare figures. Adding either is the advisor’s call, and it earns a new row, because it changes the prompt.
  • Filing. The advisor reads the draft, signs it, and files it with the model id, the date and the prompt’s sha256.
