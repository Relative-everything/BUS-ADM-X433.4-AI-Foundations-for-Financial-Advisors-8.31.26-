The Prompts, the Model, the Checks and the Failure

Two prompts produced the two drafts. The transcript the tool saw was not the transcript in section 2: the advisor cut three passages first, and every check after that was made against the turn a row cites.

Transcript→Advisor withholds 3 passages→Task A: task list→Checks 1 to 5→Advisor corrects the list→Task B: email→Checks 4 to 6→Advisor decides and sends

What never reached the tool

Three passages were cut before anything was pasted: the last three sentences of turn 2, all of turn 3, and three words of turn 5. Each is marked [WITHHELD] in the transcript the tool saw, and turn 4 records the decision in the advisor’s own words: “those stay in the file, not in any tool.” The marks have a cost. A tool cannot carry what it was never shown, and the second scenario in row 5 is where that cost lands.

Each capture was one message: the standing instruction, then the transcript, then, for the email, the checked list, then one task. The checks that were run

  1. Source lines. A script pulled every quoted fragment from the task table and looked it up in the turns the row cites. Four fragments, three traced. The one that did not was David’s “I had no idea.”, which is in turn 5 and sat under a row that cites turn 4.
  2. Types. Read each row’s type against its turn. A copy that was on file before the meeting is not a changed fact, and the draft filed one as if it were.
  3. Owners and dates. Traced each owner and date to the words that give it: the Advisor from “I want to re-run” (turn 4); David and “this week” from “I will pull the current form this week” (turn 5). The 529 rows have neither, and the table says so. The form task also said David would “get it to the advisor”; he agreed only to pull it.
  4. Nothing added. A script listed every figure and year in both drafts and looked each up in the transcript and the checked list: 6 in the task list and 4 in the email, all found, with “$3,000” for “three thousand” and “$500” for “five hundred”. It found no calendar date. Reading found one purpose with no turn behind it, in the email.
  5. Confidences. Searched both drafts and both prompts for the 29 words that appear in the withheld passages and nowhere else in the transcript or the instructions. The prompts and the task list had none. The email had one, “whether”, in its restatement of Meg’s question about the 529: a common word, not a leak.
  6. Both chairs. Read the email as Meg, then as David. Meg’s question was written about her in the third person, in a message she is reading.

Only the type and the turn number are wrong, and only a reader who opens turn 4 and looks for the quotation finds that out.

Would the model have caught it? I put that to a fresh run: the same instruction and transcript, the table, and the words “Is this task list correct? Check every row against the transcript and say what, if anything, is wrong.” (capture c1, claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-12, prompt sha256 bc8277afd08c, three runs). All three found the wrong turn number, and all three said “and get it to the advisor” went beyond what David agreed to. None noticed that a copy already on file had been filed as a changed fact, and all three advised cutting or reworking “and not just the 2029 plan”, which, cut, would have deleted a condition David set. A second look from the model is a useful extra sieve. It is not the check, because it cannot tell which of its own objections the advisor should act on.

How it was caught: check 1, by looking each quotation up in its cited turn. What changed: Task A gained three sentences: “Quote only from the turn or turns a row cites, and cite every turn a row quotes. A reply such as ‘I had no idea’ is a reaction, not an item. A document already on file is not a changed fact; put what the turns say about it on the task that needs it.” Re-run three times with that change (capture a3, claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-12, prompt sha256 5dde6f0c48af), the row was gone in all three, and the 2009 detail sat on the task with turns 4 and 5 cited.

What the change did not fix: in none of the three re-runs does a row name the second scenario David added in turn 5, and one of them says why: it left out the rest of his sentence because it ran into a withheld passage. That is the cost of the marks, not a drafting slip, and it is why the filed list names the scenario by hand.

Also hit, on the first plain requests

  • The task list (capture a1, claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-12, prompt sha256 f271ac267f45, the words “Turn the transcript above into CRM tasks for the advisor” and nothing else) ranked every task by priority, turned David’s “this week” into “Fri 10/16” using the machine’s clock, and wrote two tasks nobody agreed to: “529 adequacy review for Ryan” and “Model cost of mother’s possible assisted-living move”.
  • The email (capture b1, claude-sonnet-5-5, 2026-10-12, prompt sha256 ae9c3ec5e3b7, the words “Write a follow-up email to the clients from the advisor” and nothing else) promised to “confirm the change in writing with the plan administrator”, asked for a reply “by [date, e.g., October 23]”, proposed a 45-minute meeting “in about [two to three weeks]”, answered Meg’s tax question from general knowledge, told the clients it was keeping “sensitive family details” out of software tools, and ended with drafting notes about the withheld passages.
  • The standing instruction answers these: item 1 makes the transcript the only source, item 2 stops invented owners, dates and priorities, item 3 shuts the withheld passages, item 4 forbids answers, and item 5 stops the machine clock.

Where the human decides

  • What stays out of the tool. Three passages were withheld before anything was pasted, and turn 4 records the decision. Whether and how to record them is the advisor’s call; none appears in a task, a note or the email.
  • What the tool was never shown. The scenario David added in turn 5 rests on one of the withheld passages, so no draft can name it. The advisor writes “a second scenario David asked for” by hand and keeps the reason in the file.
  • Who owns Meg’s question. No turn names an owner for the 529 question, so the list says “owner not given” and the email promises nothing. An advisor who takes it adds one sentence to the email and a task with their own owner and date.
  • What the email says about Ruth. The list keeps Meg’s report about her mother as a note. Whether an email says anything about it is the advisor’s call; the draft said nothing, and the filed version leaves it that way.
  • Sending. The advisor signs and sends, after whatever review the firm requires.
